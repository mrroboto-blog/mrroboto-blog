# Reloading, Boating, Aviation & Survival Fundamentals: Offline Reference Guides

## Guide 1: Ammunition Reloading (Handloading) Fundamentals

Critical safety note up front: this document covers the general process, equipment, and terminology only. It does NOT contain specific powder charge weights for any cartridge — load data (powder type/charge weight/bullet/primer combination) must always come from a current, official source: the powder manufacturer's published load data (Hodgdon's free online Reloading Data Center at hodgdonreloading.com, or Hornady/Lyman/Sierra/Nosler's published manuals), cross-checked against your specific components. Never use load data from forums, memory, or estimation — an incorrect/over-maximum charge can destroy a firearm and cause severe injury or death. Always start at the published starting (minimum) load and work up gradually while watching for pressure signs, never starting at or above a published maximum.

### 1. Why Reload

- Cost savings over factory ammunition.
- Ability to tune a load for accuracy in a specific firearm.
- Self-sufficiency / supply resilience when commercial ammunition is scarce, as long as you already have a verified safe, published load and correct components in stock.

### 2. Core Equipment

- Reloading press: single-stage (one operation at a time, beginner-friendly, used for load development) vs. progressive (all steps per handle pull, higher volume, recommended only after learning fundamentals on a single-stage).
- Dies (sized per caliber): typically a 3-die set for straight-wall pistol (size/de-prime, expand, seat) or 2-die set for bottleneck rifle (size/de-prime, seat).
- Powder scale (digital, 0.1-grain resolution typical) — verify calibration with check weights before every session.
- Powder trickler/dispenser: adds powder in small increments to hit an exact target weight.
- Case prep tools: case trimmer, chamfer/deburr tool, primer pocket cleaner.
- Calipers: measure overall cartridge length (OAL) and case length against published spec.
- Priming tool: seats primers to correct depth (slightly below flush, never proud).
- Chronograph (optional): compares actual velocity to published data as a cross-check.

### 3. The Process (general outline)

1. Inspect brass: discard any case with cracks, splits, bulges, or prior pressure-problem signs.
2. Clean brass (tumbler/ultrasonic).
3. Size and de-prime: run through sizing die, which also punches out the old primer.
4. Trim to length if needed, chamfer/deburr the case mouth.
5. Prime: seat a new primer of the correct type (small/large, pistol/rifle, standard/magnum — must match published load data exactly).
6. Charge with powder: weigh the exact charge from verified, published load data. Double-check every charge — a double charge is one of the most common causes of catastrophic firearm failure. Visually inspect powder level in a tray of charged cases before seating bullets.
7. Seat the bullet to the OAL specified in your load data.
8. Crimp if applicable.
9. Inspect and gauge finished rounds.
10. Label and log: record exact components (brass lot, powder/charge, primer, bullet, OAL) and date for every batch.

### 4. Pressure Signs to Watch For (when working up a load within published min/max)

- Flattened or cratered primers, or sharp firing-pin-hole edges.
- Difficult case extraction / case head expansion beyond spec.
- Velocity significantly above published data for your stated charge.
- If you see any of these signs, stop immediately and re-examine your process.

### 5. Storage

- Store powder and primers separately, in original containers, cool/dry, away from ignition sources, following manufacturer storage guidance and local regulations.
- Keep loaded ammunition and components away from children, properly labeled.

### 6. Where to Get Actual Load Data (required reading)

- Hodgdon Reloading Data Center (hodgdonreloading.com) — free, official.
- Printed manuals from Hornady, Sierra, Nosler, Lyman.
- Always cross-reference at least two sources, and use data specific to your exact bullet weight/type and case.

## Guide 2: Basic Boat Operation Guide

General recreational boating fundamentals. Most jurisdictions require a boating safety course/license — check local requirements. This is a refresher/overview, not a substitute for that course or on-water instruction.

### 1. Pre-Departure Checklist

- Check weather/water conditions before leaving.
- Verify required safety gear: USCG-approved life jackets (one per person), fire extinguisher, visual distress signals, sound-producing device, throwable flotation device.
- File a float plan with someone on land: route, expected return time, people aboard, vessel description.
- Check fuel level, bilge for water, drain plug in.

### 2. Starting & Basic Controls

- Throttle: controls engine speed/power, usually combined with gear shift.
- Steering wheel/tiller: turns the outboard/stern drive or rudder — needs some forward motion/throttle to be effective.
- Trim (outboards/stern drives): down helps acceleration/low-speed handling, up reduces drag/increases top speed but too much can cause porpoising.
- Start in neutral, check for water discharge (confirms cooling works) before engaging gear.

### 3. Basic Maneuvering

- Boats have no brakes — stop via neutral/reverse, plan stopping distance in advance.
- Pivot point is roughly amidships or slightly aft — predicts how bow/stern swing when turning.
- Wind and current affect slow-moving boats significantly — account for drift near docks/other vessels.
- Docking approach: slow, into wind/current when possible, fenders/lines ready before close, use short reverse bursts to stop rather than drifting.

### 4. Navigation Rules (simplified)

- Head-on meeting: both alter course to starboard, pass port-to-port.
- Crossing situation: vessel with the other on its starboard side is "give-way" and must act early/obviously; the other is "stand-on" and holds course/speed.
- Overtaking: overtaking vessel must keep clear regardless of side.
- Sailing vessels, vessels fishing, and vessels not under command generally have right-of-way over power-driven vessels — know your waterway's hierarchy.
- Navigation lights (sunset-sunrise, restricted visibility): red = port, green = starboard, white = stern/all-around depending on vessel.

### 5. Person Overboard (PIW) Procedure

1. Shout "Person overboard!" and point continuously, don't lose visual contact.
2. Throw a flotation device toward them.
3. Bring the boat around keeping them in sight, approach slowly from downwind/down-current.
4. Cut the engine before anyone enters the water or the person is pulled alongside.
5. Recover from the stern/swim platform or use a ladder.

### 6. Emergency Basics

- Marine VHF Channel 16 is the international distress/hailing channel — monitor while underway. Mayday format: "Mayday, Mayday, Mayday, this is [vessel name], [position], [nature of distress], [number aboard], over."
- Know how to operate the bilge pump (manual backup if electric fails).
- Understand proper anchoring (scope typically 5-7x depth for all-chain rode, more for rope) in case of engine failure.

## Guide 3: Aircraft Emergency Familiarization (Airplane & Helicopter)

This is NOT flight training. It is the same kind of "in case the pilot becomes incapacitated" familiarization taught to non-pilot passengers in programs like AOPA's Pinch Hitter course — general knowledge of controls, radio communication, and emergency-landing concepts, intended to improve survival odds in a worst-case scenario, not to teach you to fly. The single best supplement to this document is an actual in-person Pinch Hitter-style course or a few hours of dual instruction.

Official, free, authoritative source material: the FAA's Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge and Helicopter Flying Handbook are both public-domain US government publications, free from faa.gov.

### Part 1: Airplane (small, single-engine, fixed-wing)

#### Primary Controls

- Yoke/stick: pitch (forward = nose down, back = nose up) and roll (bank left/right).
- Rudder pedals: yaw control, also used for ground steering and turn coordination.
- Throttle: engine power.
- Trim wheel/lever: relieves control pressure for hands-off flight at a given attitude.

#### If the Pilot Is Incapacitated

1. Fly the airplane first: most small aircraft are inherently stable — keep wings roughly level, avoid large/sudden inputs.
2. Communicate: set radio to 121.5 MHz (universal emergency frequency) and transmit "Mayday, Mayday, Mayday, [situation, souls on board, aircraft type if known], need assistance." ATC will talk you through everything else.
3. Set transponder to 7700 (universal emergency squawk) if you can identify it.
4. Follow ATC instructions exactly — they can see your position/altitude and give step-by-step guidance including nearest suitable airport.
5. Keep the horizon level across the windscreen, maintain gentle consistent pitch, small smooth corrections only.

### Part 2: Helicopter

#### Primary Controls

- Cyclic: tilts the main rotor disk, controls horizontal movement direction.
- Collective: changes pitch of all main rotor blades simultaneously, controls lift/altitude (twist-grip throttle often built in on piston helicopters).
- Anti-torque pedals: control yaw/nose direction via tail rotor thrust.

#### Autorotation (key emergency concept)

- On engine failure, the main rotor autorotates (spins freely from upward airflow) during a controlled descent and still provides lift if flown correctly.
- Lowering the collective promptly to maintain rotor RPM is a trained reflex with a very narrow timing window — this is conceptual knowledge only, not something a non-pilot should attempt solo. Helicopter emergencies are substantially less survivable without a trained pilot at the controls compared to fixed-wing.
- If a helicopter pilot is incapacitated, the realistic priority is the same communication steps as above.

#### Communication (same as airplane)

- 121.5 MHz emergency frequency, "Mayday" format, 7700 transponder code if accessible.

### Part 3: General Emergency Landing Concepts (fixed-wing, conceptual)

- Best glide speed: the published airspeed that maximizes distance per altitude lost during engine-out — maintaining it is the single most important factor in reaching a safe landing area.
- Landing area selection: long, flat, open areas preferred (roads, fields), avoid trees/powerlines/buildings, land into the wind when possible.
- Communicate intentions to ATC/radio throughout so help can be directed to your landing location.

### Where to Get the Full Official Handbooks

- FAA Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge — faa.gov, free PDF, public domain.
- FAA Helicopter Flying Handbook — faa.gov, free PDF, public domain.
- AOPA's Pinch Hitter course materials (aopa.org) — purpose-built for this exact topic.

## Guide 4: Essential Knots & Survival Basics

### Part 1: Knots (the practical "must-know" set)

Learn these on actual rope/cord, not just by reading.

#### Stopper / Joining Knots

- Square knot (reef knot): joins two similar-diameter ropes. Not load-bearing/trustworthy for critical loads — good for bundling, bandaging, non-critical tie-offs.
- Sheet bend: joins two ropes including different diameters — more reliable than a square knot for mismatched cordage.
- Figure-8 knot: a stopper knot and the base for the figure-8 follow-through (strong climbing-rated loop).

#### Loop Knots

- Bowline: forms a fixed loop that doesn't slip/jam under load yet unties easily afterward — the classic rescue loop knot.
- Alpine butterfly: secure mid-line loop loadable from multiple directions.

#### Hitches

- Clove hitch: quick to tie/adjust around a post, can slip under steady one-directional load — good for temporary/adjustable tie-offs only.
- Taut-line hitch: adjustable loop that grips under load but slides to change tension — standard for tent/tarp guy-lines.
- Trucker's hitch: creates a mechanical-advantage pulley using only rope, for cinching loads/tensioning a ridge line.

#### Shelter/Lashing

- Prusik knot: friction hitch that grips a thicker rope under load but slides when unweighted — used for ascending, safety backup, adjustable shelter lines.
- Square lashing: binds two poles at a right angle using wraps and frapping turns.

### Part 2: Survival Basics (Rule of 3s priority order)

Roughly: 3 minutes without air/severe bleeding control, 3 hours without shelter in harsh conditions, 3 days without water, 3 weeks without food — sets the priority order below.

#### 1. Immediate Safety & Bleeding

- Stop severe bleeding first (direct pressure, elevate, tourniquet for life-threatening limb bleeding if direct pressure fails).
- Assess immediate environmental dangers before anything else.

#### 2. Shelter & Core Temperature

- Hypothermia is a faster killer than expected, even in mild-seeming conditions (40-50°F with wet/wind) — get out of wet clothing, insulate from the ground, build/find shelter before dark.
- Shelter priorities: off the ground, block wind, retain body heat.
- Heat-related illness in hot climates: seek shade, slow pace, hydrate, recognize heat exhaustion before it progresses to heat stroke (medical emergency).

#### 3. Signaling

- Three of anything is the universal distress signal (fires, whistle blasts, light flashes), arranged in a triangle for ground signals.
- Signal mirrors visible for miles in good sunlight.
- Ground-to-air signals: large "X" (need medical assistance) or "V" (need assistance) from contrasting material.
- Decide early: stay put and signal (best if someone knows your plan) vs. self-rescue by moving.

#### 4. Water

- Never ration water if you have a source you can treat — drink to meet needs and focus on finding/treating more.
- Treatment methods by reliability: boiling (1 min rolling boil, longer at altitude), chemical treatment (iodine/chlorine dioxide), filtration (most filters don't remove viruses — check rated pore size).
- Water sources best to worst: moving over standing, deeper/larger over shallow, away from obvious contamination — but treat anything regardless of appearance.

#### 5. Fire

- Serves shelter, water treatment, signaling, food prep, morale.
- Fire triangle: heat + fuel + oxygen. Build in stages: tinder, kindling, fuelwood.
- Carry redundant fire-starting methods: ferro rod, waterproof matches, lighter.

#### 6. Food (lowest priority of the core four)

- Covered in the dedicated hunting/fishing and foraging guides — prioritize lower-effort calorie sources over high-effort/high-risk pursuits.

### Part 3: Navigation Basics

- A map and compass need no batteries — learn orienting the map, taking a bearing, following it.
- Without a compass: an analog watch pointed with the hour hand at the sun gives a rough north-south line (northern hemisphere, bisect angle to 12:00); the North Star (Polaris), found via the Big Dipper's pointer stars, sits almost exactly at true north.

## Glossary

Plain-language explanations of the specialized terms used above:

**Reloading (Guide 1)**
- **Handloading / reloading**: assembling your own ammunition from individual components (brass, primer, powder, bullet) instead of buying factory-loaded rounds.
- **Press (single-stage/progressive)**: the tool that performs reloading steps. A single-stage press does one operation at a time (good for learning); a progressive press performs multiple steps per handle pull (faster, for experienced reloaders).
- **Dies**: precision tools, matched to a specific cartridge, that resize brass, seat primers/bullets, etc.
- **OAL (overall length)**: the total length of a finished, assembled cartridge, which must match published specifications.
- **Primer**: the small component that ignites the powder charge when struck by the firing pin.
- **Case head expansion**: a cartridge brass case swelling in an unsafe way from excess pressure — a warning sign to stop.
- **Chronograph**: a device that measures a bullet's velocity as it leaves the barrel.
- **Grain**: a unit of weight used for gunpowder and bullets (far smaller than a gram).

**Boating (Guide 2)**
- **Float plan**: a written summary of your trip (route, return time, people aboard) left with someone on land in case of emergency.
- **Bilge**: the lowest inner part of a boat's hull, where water collects; the "bilge pump" removes it.
- **Trim**: adjusting the angle of an outboard motor/drive relative to the water to optimize handling or speed.
- **Porpoising**: an unstable, bouncing motion of a boat's bow caused by excessive trim at speed.
- **Amidships**: the middle section of a boat (front-to-back).
- **Give-way / stand-on vessel**: in navigation rules, the "give-way" vessel must maneuver to avoid collision; the "stand-on" vessel should hold its course/speed.
- **PIW (person in water)**: the boating term/procedure for a person-overboard emergency.
- **VHF Channel 16**: the international marine radio channel monitored for distress calls and hailing other vessels.
- **Scope / rode** (anchoring): "scope" is the ratio of anchor-line length to water depth; "rode" is the anchor line/chain itself.

**Aviation (Guide 3)**
- **Yoke / cyclic / collective**: the primary flight controls — a yoke (airplane) or cyclic (helicopter) tilts the aircraft's direction of travel; a helicopter's collective controls overall lift/altitude.
- **Rudder pedals / anti-torque pedals**: foot controls for yaw (nose-left/right) — called anti-torque pedals on a helicopter because they counteract the main rotor's spin.
- **ATC (Air Traffic Control)**: ground-based controllers who direct and assist aircraft by radio.
- **Transponder / squawk code**: an aircraft's radio device that broadcasts its identity/altitude to controllers; "squawking 7700" signals a general emergency.
- **121.5 MHz**: the international VHF emergency/distress radio frequency monitored by aircraft and controllers.
- **Autorotation**: a helicopter's ability to glide safely to a landing using airflow spinning the rotor freely after engine failure, rather than engine power.
- **RPM (revolutions per minute)**: rotor/engine spin speed, which must be kept within a safe range.
- **Best glide speed**: the specific airspeed that lets a powerless aircraft travel the farthest while losing the least altitude.
- **AOPA / Pinch Hitter**: the Aircraft Owners and Pilots Association's course teaching non-pilots basic emergency skills in case a pilot is incapacitated.

**Knots & survival (Guide 4)**
- **Frapping turns**: extra wraps added around a lashing to tighten and lock it in place.
- **Ferro rod**: a fire-starting tool (a rod of ferrocerium metal) that throws sparks when scraped with a hard edge.
- **Rule of 3s**: a survival-priority mnemonic (roughly: 3 minutes without air, 3 hours without shelter, 3 days without water, 3 weeks without food).
- **Polaris (North Star)**: the star that sits almost directly above the Earth's north pole, used for navigation without a compass.
