# Self-Defense, Home Safety & Martial Arts Fundamentals: Offline Reference Guides

## Goal

Add three more offline reference guides to the bug-out USB's knowledge base, covering
personal and family safety: unarmed self-defense fundamentals, a family home-safety/
sheltering plan, and the grappling/striking fundamentals behind two widely-taught combat
sports (BJJ and Muay Thai). As with the mesh/hunting/foraging guides, these are meant to
be read and practiced ahead of time — none of this substitutes for in-person instruction.

## Guide 1: Personal Self-Defense Fundamentals

This is a foundational reference, not a substitute for in-person training. Self-defense is a perishable physical skill — find a local reputable instructor (Krav Maga, Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, boxing, or a dedicated women's/general self-defense course) to actually drill these under supervision.

### 1. Awareness & Avoidance (the most effective "technique")

- Most self-defense is won before any physical contact: stay off your phone in transition spaces (parking lots, stairwells), park/walk in lit areas, trust your gut if a situation feels wrong, and vary routines/routes if you feel watched/followed.
- De-escalation: create distance, keep hands up open/visible (non-aggressive posture that's also ready to defend), use a loud firm verbal command ("BACK UP," "STOP") — this also attracts witnesses/help.
- Identify exits and improvised barriers/weapons (keys, pen, flashlight, everyday objects) in any space you regularly occupy.

### 2. Core Physical Principles

- Target the attacker's weaknesses, not their strength: eyes, throat, groin, knees, instep. You don't need to "win a fight," you need to create enough damage/shock to escape.
- Base and balance: a strong stance (feet shoulder-width, knees soft, weight centered) is the foundation for every strike and every escape.
- Use your whole body, not just an arm: hip rotation and leg drive generate real striking power.
- Noise matters: yelling while striking helps generate power and draws attention/help.

### 3. Basic Strikes (safe, high-percentage, low skill-ceiling)

- Palm-heel strike to the nose/chin: safer for your own hand than a closed fist, very effective at close range.
- Elbow strikes: extremely effective at very close range, hard to defend against.
- Knee strikes: to the groin/thigh when grabbed or clinched.
- Eye jab/poke: a last-resort disabling strike if grabbed/mounted.

### 4. Escaping Common Grabs/Holds

- Wrist grab: rotate your wrist toward the attacker's thumb (the weakest point of their grip) while pulling your arm back and stepping to create angle.
- Bear hug (arms pinned): drop your base (bend knees, widen stance) to prevent being lifted, then strike groin/stomp instep, then create distance immediately.
- Bear hug (arms free): strike first (palm-heel/elbow to face), then disengage.
- Choke from front: both hands up, chin tucked, turn your head/body to the weak side of their grip while striking with your free hand/knee.
- Mount/ground pin: "buck and roll" — trap one of their arms/legs, bridge your hips explosively while rolling toward the trapped side to reverse position or create space to escape — the goal is standing and running, not winning a ground fight.

### 5. Weapon Awareness (not weapon-use training — requires in-person instruction)

- If a weapon is present during a robbery demand, compliance is usually the statistically safer choice.
- If an attack with a weapon is already happening, control the weapon-bearing limb/wrist with both hands while creating angle and striking — dedicated knife-defense training (in person) is strongly recommended over relying on a guide.

### 6. After Any Incident

- Get to a safe, populated location and call emergency services.
- Note details while fresh: description, direction of travel, vehicle/plate info.
- Seek medical evaluation even for "minor" injuries.

### 7. Practice Plan

- Shadow-drill the strikes above for form, focusing on hip rotation.
- Find a local class with live, resisting partner drills — this is the part a written guide cannot replace.

## Guide 2: Family Home-Safety & Sheltering Plan (Run-Hide-Fight Framework)

This is an individual/family-oriented home-safety plan, built on the widely-taught civilian "Run-Hide-Fight" framework (used in active-threat training by DHS/FBI and many corporate/school safety programs). It is about protecting your household, not team tactical movement.

### 1. Run (first choice, whenever possible)

- If a threat is outside or you can safely exit, leave immediately — don't gather belongings.
- Pre-plan two exit routes from every main room in your home, practiced with family members including kids.
- Establish a family rally point outside the home so everyone regroups in one known place.
- Keep exit paths clear of clutter; keep a shoes/flashlight/phone-charger "grab kit" near primary exits.

### 2. Hide / Shelter (if you can't safely run)

- Identify your home's safe room in advance: ideally an interior room, ground floor if possible, with a lockable solid-core door, no exterior windows.
- Harden that room ahead of time: solid-core/reinforced door, a door wedge/security bar, door-frame reinforcement kit.
- When sheltering: lock/barricade the door, turn off lights, silence phones (keep accessible to call 911), stay low and away from door/windows, stay quiet.
- Keep a small kit in the safe room: phone charger/battery bank, flashlight, basic first aid, water.

### 3. Fight (absolute last resort, only if Run/Hide aren't possible and your life is in immediate danger)

- This means defending yourself/family in the moment, not moving to confront a threat elsewhere in the home.
- Improvised tools: fire extinguisher (discharge toward face at close range, then use as blunt object), heavy flashlight, bat/golf club kept in the safe room.
- Commit fully and look for the first opportunity to flee or create an opening to escape, then run.

### 4. Communication Plan

- Agree on a code word with family members that signals "get to the safe room / leave now" without alarming an intruder.
- Program emergency contacts into every family member's phone.
- Decide who calls 911 by default so calls aren't duplicated and someone stays on the line.
- When calling 911: give address first, then threat description, then your location in the home.

### 5. Physical Hardening (do this regardless of threat likelihood)

- Deadbolts on all exterior doors, strike-plate screws long enough to reach the stud — the single highest-value, lowest-cost home security upgrade.
- Motion-sensor exterior lighting at entrances.
- Consider a basic alarm/camera system.
- Know your neighbors.

### 6. Practice

- Walk through Run and Hide plans with the whole family at least twice a year.
- Re-walk exit routes after any home layout changes.

## Guide 3: Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu & Muay Thai Fundamentals

A technique reference for two complementary combat sports: BJJ (ground-based grappling/submissions) and Muay Thai (striking with fists, elbows, knees, shins — "the art of eight limbs"). Like the self-defense guide, this is a reference for studying concepts between classes, not a substitute for in-person coaching — live, resisting training partners are essential to actually develop these skills.

### Part 1: Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (BJJ)

#### Core Philosophy

Position before submission. Control a dominant position first; the submission follows naturally once control is established.

#### Base Positions (best to worst control, roughly)

1. Back mount (back control) — hooks in or body triangle, both arms controlling the upper body/neck. The most dominant position in BJJ.
2. Mount — straddling the opponent's torso, knees pinched for control.
3. Side control — perpendicular to the opponent, chest-to-chest pressure.
4. Knee-on-belly — mobile pressure position, good for transitions.
5. Guard (closed/open) — on your back with legs controlling the opponent's hips/posture.
6. Half guard — one of your legs trapped between the opponent's legs.

#### Fundamental Escapes

- Mount escape (upa/bridge-and-roll): trap one arm and the same-side leg, bridge your hips explosively toward that side, roll the opponent over into your guard.
- Mount escape (elbow-knee/shrimp): create a frame with your forearm across the opponent's hip/thigh, shrimp (hip escape) your hips out to recover guard.
- Side control escape: frame against the near shoulder/hip, shrimp to create space, recover guard or get to your knees.

#### Fundamental Submissions

- Armbar: isolate one arm, control it straight across your body with hip pressure, extend their elbow against the joint's natural range.
- Rear naked choke (from back control): arm under the chin, hand gripped to the opposite bicep/shoulder, other hand behind the head — the single highest-percentage submission in the sport.
- Triangle choke (from guard): trap one arm and the head between your legs, lock your legs into a figure-four, squeeze using your legs/hips.
- Kimura (shoulder lock): figure-four grip on the wrist/arm, rotate the shoulder — effective from multiple positions.

#### Fundamental Sweeps (reversing position from guard)

- Scissor sweep: off-balance the opponent using a scissoring leg motion combined with an arm drag/pull, rolling them over to end up on top.
- Hip bump sweep: sit up explosively off your guard, drive your hip into theirs while posting your hand, rolling them backward.

### Part 2: Muay Thai

#### Stance & Guard

Bladed stance (lead shoulder/foot pointed at opponent), hands up protecting the chin, elbows tucked to protect the ribs.

#### The Eight Limbs (strikes)

- Jab/cross (hands): range-finders and setups more than fight-enders in Muay Thai compared to boxing.
- Teep (push kick): a straight kick with the ball/heel of the foot to the hips/midsection — used defensively to maintain range as much as offensively.
- Roundhouse kick (the signature Muay Thai weapon): rotate the hip and supporting foot fully through the kick, striking with the shin, not the foot/instep.
- Elbow strikes: horizontal, downward (spiking), and upward (uppercut) elbows at close range — among the highest-damage strikes in the sport.
- Knee strikes: straight knee and curving knee, devastatingly effective especially from the clinch.

#### The Clinch

Controlling the opponent's head/neck (double collar tie or single-side control) to off-balance them and land knee strikes. Fundamentals: control the inside position, off-balance by pulling the head down/to an angle, strike with knees while maintaining control, know basic clinch escapes (underhooks, framing, circling off the edge).

#### Defense Fundamentals

- Checking kicks: raise the shin/knee to block an incoming roundhouse kick with your own shin.
- Parrying/catching teeps: redirect or catch a push kick to off-balance the kicker.
- Head movement + guard: less head movement than boxing (due to kick/knee/elbow threats) but still relies on a tight guard and moving off centerline.

### Training Note

Both arts are best learned at a reputable gym with qualified instruction and live partner drilling. This document is useful for reviewing concepts between sessions, not for learning the physical skill in isolation.

## Glossary

Plain-language explanations of the specialized terms used above:

**Self-defense (Guide 1)**
- **De-escalation**: using words, distance, and body language to calm or defuse a confrontation before it turns physical.
- **Krav Maga**: an Israeli military-derived self-defense system focused on practical, fast-ending techniques.
- **Base and balance**: a stable body position (feet planted, knees bent, weight centered) that makes you harder to knock down and lets you strike with more power.
- **Palm-heel strike**: a strike using the heel of an open palm instead of a closed fist, which reduces the risk of hand injury.

**Home safety (Guide 2)**
- **Run-Hide-Fight**: a civilian active-threat safety framework (taught by US agencies like DHS/FBI) describing, in priority order, your options when facing an armed/violent threat.
- **Safe room**: a pre-identified, hardened interior room used for sheltering during a threat.
- **Solid-core door**: a door made of solid wood/composite rather than hollow, which resists forced entry far better.

**BJJ & Muay Thai (Guide 3)**
- **BJJ (Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu)**: a ground-grappling martial art focused on controlling an opponent and applying joint locks/chokes ("submissions").
- **Mount / side control / guard / half guard / knee-on-belly**: named body positions in BJJ describing who is controlling whom and how, ranked roughly from most to least dominant.
- **Submission**: a grappling technique (joint lock or choke) that forces an opponent to "tap out" (surrender) to avoid injury or unconsciousness.
- **Armbar / rear naked choke / triangle choke / kimura**: specific named BJJ submission techniques targeting an arm, the airway/blood flow to the brain, or a shoulder joint.
- **Sweep**: a BJJ technique that reverses position from the bottom to the top.
- **Muay Thai**: a Thai striking martial art nicknamed "the art of eight limbs" for its use of fists, elbows, knees, and shins.
- **Bladed stance**: standing at an angle (rather than square) to the opponent, presenting a smaller target.
- **Teep**: a straight push-kick used to control distance in Muay Thai.
- **Roundhouse kick**: a circular kick that strikes with the shin.
- **Clinch**: a close-range grappling position (standing, holding the opponent's head/neck) used in Muay Thai to control and strike with knees.
- **IBJJF**: the International Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu Federation, a major organization that standardizes BJJ competition rules/terminology.
